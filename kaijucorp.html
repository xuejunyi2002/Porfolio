About the Project

Project Introduction

Kaiju Corp. is a self-directed asymmetric multiplayer game currently being developed by VGS Studio. The project explores how gaze, hand gestures, and responsive feedback can create more natural and intentional interaction in VR.

The game connects a VR player and PC players within the same experience. The VR player takes on the role of a kaiju with embodied attack and interaction abilities, while PC players move through the environment, fight back, and work toward their own objectives.

Rather than relying heavily on menus or traditional pointer-based controls, the project investigates how gaze and gesture can help the VR player select targets and perform actions more naturally.

Unlike a client-based production, Kaiju Corp. is developed and directed entirely by our venture team. This means we are responsible for defining the vision, validating the concept, managing the scope, creating the production plan, and making creative and product decisions throughout development.

As Project Manager and Producer, I lead the production process across iterative planning, scope management, team coordination, playtesting, retrospectives, and external art collaboration. The project is currently in active production and is scheduled for delivery in August 2026.

Key Challenges

Problem → Response → Current Outcome

Kaiju Corp. is both an experimental interaction project and a self-directed multiplayer game production. Without an external client setting requirements, the team must continuously evaluate its own vision, scope, priorities, and evidence. As Project Manager and Producer, my focus is to give iteration enough flexibility to support discovery while maintaining the structure required to deliver a cohesive game in August.

Challenge
My Response
Current Outcome
01 — Managing Iterative Production

The project changes quickly as prototypes, technical discoveries, team discussions, and player feedback reveal new information. A fixed long-term plan can quickly stop reflecting the reality of the production.

I organize development through two-week cycles, a prioritized backlog, milestone reviews, and regular retrospectives. New findings and ideas are evaluated through player value, production effort, dependencies, technical risk, and alignment with the core experience before they become committed tasks.

The team can adapt without losing its production direction. Iteration is managed through a visible decision-making process rather than continuous and uncontrolled change.

02 — Protecting Scope in a Self-Directed Project

Because the team owns the creative direction, there are many possible ideas we could explore. We are also developing both an experimental interaction system and a complete VR + PC game within the capacity of a four-person core team.

I help the team maintain a clear distinction between the core playable loop, interaction requirements, and exploratory ideas. The roadmap and backlog are regularly reviewed so that new ideas do not automatically become production commitments.

The project can retain its experimental and creative value while continuing to move toward a cohesive, playable, and deliverable game experience.

03 — Coordinating External Art Collaborators

External art collaborators work outside the core team and have different schedules, availability, and production capacity. Assignments that are too large, unclear, or dependent on unfinished work could create delays and integration bottlenecks.

I divide the art scope into prioritized, self-contained assignments and match them to each contributor's strengths and availability. I provide clear references, expectations, ownership, and review points, while tracking asset creation separately from Unity integration.

External collaborators can contribute through realistic commitments, while the core team maintains visibility over asset progress, review status, and integration readiness.

Leadership Principles

What I Carry Forward

01

Iteration Needs Structure

An iterative project still requires a stable decision-making framework. Short production cycles, retrospectives, milestones, and success criteria allow the plan to evolve while keeping the team aligned around the intended experience and delivery target.

02

Scope Is an Ongoing Decision

Scope is not defined once and left unchanged. In an experimental game project, it must be continuously evaluated against evidence, team capacity, dependencies, and production risk. My role is to preserve the value of exploration while protecting the team's ability to deliver.

03

Flexible Collaboration Requires Clear Boundaries

External contributors do not need to follow the core team's exact workflow, but they need clear priorities, ownership, references, review points, and handoff expectations. Flexible collaboration works best when the boundaries around each contribution are explicit.
